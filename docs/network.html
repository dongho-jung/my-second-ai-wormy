Deep reinforcement learning · PPO · self-play

How the worm decides

Sixty times a second the game moves. Fifteen times a second a network looks at it and presses keys. This is everything that network sees, everything it computes, and everything it says — with the real shapes at every step.

What to call it

It is deep reinforcement learning. Nobody labels the right answer; the worm acts, the game pays or charges it, and the network is nudged toward whatever earned more.

The particular method is PPO — proximal policy optimisation — an actor-critic scheme. The actor picks keys. The critic guesses how the round is going to go, which is what turns a reward that arrives late into a signal about the move that caused it.

Self-play
All three worms in a match are the same network. Whatever one of them learns, it immediately has to face.
Behavioural cloning
Recorded human play, folded into the same updates. Reward alone never stumbles onto the rope.
Frameskip 4
One decision covers four game ticks, so the network runs at 15 Hz while the world runs at 60.

What it sees

Three tensors, rebuilt every decision. Two are pictures and go through convolutions; one is a list of numbers and goes through a dense layer.

The vector

144

Floats. Health, ammo, aim, sixteen rangefinder rays, where the other worms are and what they are holding.

The patch

4 × 32 × 32

The 64 × 64 pixels around the worm, two pixels per cell: rock, dirt, open air, and any shot in flight.

The map

4 × 32 × 32

The whole 504 × 350 level squeezed onto a grid, as fractions — plus a channel marking where everybody is.

Where the weights are

936,435 parameters, and nearly nine in ten of them sit in a single layer — the one that takes 3,264 joined numbers down to 256. Everything else is rounding.

Why it matters. If this network ever runs out of capacity, that layer is where to spend more. The convolutions are tiny by comparison, and making them wider would barely change the total.

Why seven heads and not one

The seven choices multiply out to 648 actions. A single output over all 648 would have to learn walking right and firing as 648 separate facts; seven small choices learn them once each and combine them. It is eighteen numbers instead of 648, and a policy that has learned to walk keeps that while it learns to shoot.

Two of the choices are three-way rather than pairs of bits, and that is not tidiness. Holding left and right together is how the engine digs — the same bit the dig key sets. A move head that could ask for both would be asking for something else entirely, so it cannot.

What is missing

There is no memory. The network sees the current frame and nothing before it, so it can learn to back off when hurt and to line up a shot, but it cannot hold a plan across seconds, or remember which direction it was shot from, or that somebody went behind that rock a moment ago.

That is a property of the shape above, not of how long it trains. Reactive fighting is reachable as drawn; planning is not, and would need recurrence or a stack of recent frames added to the input.

The value head currently explains about 65% of the variance in how rounds turn out. A critic that could not see what mattered would sit near zero — so what is drawn above is carrying most of what decides a match.
